# BSc Capstone Course Description (English Translation)

## Page 1

Description
This project consists of developing a realistic software project in collaboration with external partners of the university and expected end users. The capstone project is completed in groups of 2-4 students under the supervision of an instructor. Students produce a detailed project report and manuals that are delivered with the software.

The capstone is assessed by the project instructor and two examiners from Reykjavik University. Assessment is based on the factors listed below and is performed in several stages while students work on the project. The project work formally concludes with a public presentation at the university.

Note: To register for the capstone project, students must have completed at least 78 ECTS credits.

Learning Objectives
Knowledge:
- Gain experience working on a medium-sized software or research project in a team.
- Explain how to analyze user needs and implement them in software, or how to analyze partner requirements when shaping a research project.
- Explain recognized methods for software system design and analysis, or research-problem design and analysis, and select an appropriate method.
- Gain practical training in project management.

Competence:
- Form a team, create a plan, and execute the project according to that plan.
- Use version control systems in software development and/or report writing.
- Define and perform user, unit, and system testing when applicable.
- Perform risk assessment.

Skills:
- At any point, explain project status: what is complete, what remains, and how progress compares with plan.
- Present work to different audiences, including both technical and non-technical audiences.

Prerequisites:
- T-216-GHOH Software Analysis and Design
- T-220-VLN2 Practical Course 2
- T-303-HUGB Software Engineering

# Practical Information For BSc Capstone Students (English Translation)

Owner: Bjorn Thor Jonsson ([Icelandic title in source: Umsjonaradili])
Course: T-404-LOKA

## Page 1

Welcome to the BSc capstone at Reykjavik University. This document answers common questions for students starting their capstone work. Capstones may be either software projects or research projects.

The same general course process applies to both project types, although evaluation criteria differ somewhat. This document provides a general framework; exact implementation can vary significantly depending on project type. Coordinate with the course owner and/or supervisor if this general framework does not fit your case.

### 1. First Steps
Keywords: Form group, choose project, contact supervisor, find workspace, decide work hours, decide process, define scope.

#### 1.1 Form a Group
Preferred group size is 3-4. Groups of 2 or 5 may be approved.

Choose your capstone topic as early as possible (ideally before semester start). Start working with momentum from day one and maintain progress through the semester.

#### 1.2 Find a Project
You may contact companies directly or work with collaborators within RU. Most Computer Science faculty are active researchers, so contact relevant faculty if their work matches your interests.

The course owner seeks project proposals from companies and RU staff, including research and software-development projects. These proposals are open to students.

In general, software companies/departments with strong technical contacts are easiest to work with. Other company types are possible, especially when a group member knows the organization well. Startup projects are also possible, but group members should agree on product ownership in writing at project start.

All project proposals must be approved by the course owner (bjorn@ru.is).
HR capstone proposal template and examples:
https://www.ru.is/departments/tolvunarfraedideild#samstarf

#### 1.3 Contact Your Supervisor
At semester start, after group and project are selected, each group is assigned a supervisor and examiner.

Contact your supervisor immediately after assignment, confirm that work has started, and establish regular meetings. Initiative and execution speed usually affect final grade.

#### 1.4 Decide Work Setup, Hours, and Process
Set a fixed team work structure early. Establish a shared workspace if possible (at partner company or another practical location). Create a schedule showing when members can work on the project.

Set recurring weekly times when all members meet together. If some members are remote, set recurring remote meetings (for example Zoom or Teams).

Start with a defined process from day one. Do not treat the first week as undefined setup time. Define process immediately and execute it.

If Scrum is used, set up your first sprint immediately, including setup tasks (workspace, development environment, initial product backlog). Also decide estimation method early (story points vs hours).

#### 1.5 Project Scope
ECTS guideline: 1 ECTS corresponds to 25-30 work hours, so a 12 ECTS capstone implies 300-360 hours.

Work includes all project activities: process definition, preparation tasks, progress meetings and prep, and final report.

This hour range is a minimum baseline to avoid failing; quality still must satisfy supervisor and examiner. In practice, strong projects often require at least around 360 hours, sometimes more.

### 2. BSc Thesis / Final Report
Keywords: project description, risk analysis, requirements analysis, system design, UI design, progress.

The final report is a primary deliverable. It must contain enough detail for a third party to reproduce the project at a high level. It should stand on its own as an engineering and/or scientific description, without over-explaining basic CS knowledge.

Start report work at semester start and update continuously.

What belongs in the report depends on project type. Examples:
- A backend-heavy calculation/data-processing system may require deeper system design details.
- A user-facing web system may require stronger user-help/context documentation.
- Operational documentation may in some cases be better in repository `README`.
- User documentation may sometimes live in the product itself.

Evaluate documentation needs per project. Coordinate with supervisor. The final report must also be useful to customer-side staff who may need to install, maintain, or operate the system.

Review prior capstones at:
www.skemman.is (search Reykjavik University Computer Science).
Quality varies, so consult supervisor/course owner for strong examples.

Some documentation elements are always required for both research and software capstones; the next sections describe these.

## Page 4

### 2.1 Project Description
Include:
- Main project goals and expected outcome.
- Problem being solved: current state vs target state.
- Existing solutions and how your solution differs.
- Whether scope changed from original proposal.
- Technology stack.

Also include process, project plan, work organization, and other execution details.

### 2.2 Risk Analysis
Risk analysis should be project-specific and treated seriously.

Recommended rules:
- Identify project-specific risks (not only generic ones like illness/device failure).
- For each risk, define:
  1. How to reduce likelihood.
  2. How to reduce impact if it happens.
- Prioritize risks, often via 1-5 likelihood and 1-5 impact. Their product gives risk score.
- Assign a responsible owner for each risk.
- Keep a risk log with realized events, actions taken, and timestamps.
- Keep risk analysis as a living document; update likelihood/impact and reorder regularly.

## Page 5

### 2.3 Process and Plan
This section acts as a working agreement between team, partner, and supervisor.

Document your process, work hours, and execution model. Example: if using Scrum, define roles, sprint length, and meeting format. If research-focused, define phase windows (for example data collection weeks, model-building weeks).

Define clear team rules for attendance and task distribution, and define response when rules are violated.

### 2.4 Requirements / Product Backlog
Maintain project requirements in a clear structure.

Based on user and needs analysis with partner:
- Who are users?
- What must be delivered by end of project?
- How are requirements prioritized?

For Scrum teams, this is usually a prioritized (and ideally estimated) product backlog with user stories.
For research projects, this may be milestone-based checkpoints.

Keep this as a living list during project, then include a printable/final form in final deliverables.

### 2.5 System and UI Design
Likely needed in some form for most projects.

Design section commonly has two parts:
- UI/UX design
- System design

Use diagrams/figures where useful, but always reference and explain them in text.

## Page 6

### 2.6 Progress Tracking
Track progress continuously.

Include:
- Time logging per team member.
- Time split by category (design, coding, documentation, meetings, etc.).
- Progress status: what is complete and what remains.

For Scrum teams, include sprint artifacts such as sprint backlog and burndown.
Burndown requires estimates before sprint starts (hours or story points).

Also summarize planning and retrospective outcomes, especially:
- Sprint goal
- What went well
- What to improve next sprint

Reference progress against project plan each period. Detailed sprint summaries may be placed in an appendix.

### 2.7 References
Always cite sources using a recognized standard (for example APA or IEEE).

Typical baseline:
- Software project final report: at least two references.
- Research project final report: usually more references.

## Page 7

### 3. Status Meetings
There are three status meetings during project execution.

Treat them as formal reviews where you demonstrate that the project is on track.
Do not treat them as informal check-ins.

Typical format:
- 25-30 minute presentation (all team members speak)
- ~15 minutes discussion with examiner and supervisor

Core items you must always report:
- Hours spent so far (team total and per person)
- Planned hours by this date according to project plan
- Current status: done vs remaining
- Whether project is ahead or behind schedule

Meeting-specific focus:
- First status meeting: team, project idea, process choice, risk analysis.
- Second status meeting: short recap + deeper design/implementation details, prototypes/screenshots where relevant.
- Third status meeting: rehearsal for public final presentation; system should largely be complete.

Public final presentation:
Held at RU and open to public. Audience may be non-technical, so keep technical depth balanced. Main focus is to clearly show project outcome.

Planned meeting windows (as listed in source):
- First status meeting: week of Feb 9-13
- Second status meeting: week of Mar 9-13
- Third status meeting: week of Apr 27-30
- Final presentations: week of May 18-22

## Page 8

### 4. Final Submission
Submit final report to Skemman (www.skemman.is).

The report must be carefully prepared and reviewed by supervisor and examiner before submission.
Spelling, language quality, and presentation must be professional.

The final report must have clear chapter structure and table of contents.
After submission to Skemman, report and code are not editable there.
Code is not uploaded to Skemman, but supervisor and examiner must receive access/copies when final report is submitted.

### 5. Use of AI in Capstone Projects
Responsible use of AI tools is allowed, similar to industry practice.

Examples of acceptable use:
- Coding assistance after implementation intent is defined.
- Test generation support, especially unit tests.
- Help understanding legacy code.
- Language/spelling proofreading support.

Final report must state:
- How AI tools were used.
- How AI outputs were validated.

The team remains responsible for all deliverables. You must understand and verify AI-generated outputs. AI tools can introduce serious errors, especially in unusual software contexts.

Using AI to write report text is risky; output may become generic/superficial and fail to reflect your real technical understanding.

If AI reduces coding time, invest saved time into other quality-critical work, especially user testing.

### 6. General Note
A core goal of capstone is to experience real project execution. Technical complexity is only one part of evaluation.

Professional work practices, code/report clarity, presentation quality, initiative, and professional conduct across the semester all affect final grade.
